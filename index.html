How to Spot a Phishing Email

Phishing emails are fake messages that try to trick you into giving away your passwords or money. This short guide shows what to look for and what to do if you get one.

Already clicked something? Go straight to what to do.

What is phishing?

Phishing is when a scammer sends a fake message that looks like it came from a company or person you trust, like your bank, a delivery service, or your school. They want you to click a link, open a file, or give them your password or money.

It works because the messages look normal and make you feel rushed or worried. In 2025, phishing was the most-reported type of internet crime to the FBI, with 191,561 complaints (see Sources below).

6 warning signs

Here is a made-up phishing email. The numbers match the warning signs listed under it.

From: Brightline Bank Security <alerts@brightline-secure.co> Warning sign [1]

Subject: URGENT: Verify within 24 hours or your account will be closed Warning sign [2]

Dear Valued Customer, Warning sign [3]

We noticed a sign-in to your account from a new device. Please confirm your password and card details to keep your account open. Warning sign [4]

Verify my account Warning sign [5]
The link really goes to: https://brightline-secure.co/verify

Attachment: Account_Verification.html Warning sign [6]

  1. The sender’s address doesn’t match. The name says “Brightline Bank,” but the address ends in brightline-secure.co. Anyone can type any name, so check the address behind it.
  2. Urgent or scary language. Threats and short deadlines are meant to rush you so you don’t stop to think.
  3. A generic greeting. “Dear Valued Customer” suggests the same email went to lots of people. Some scammers do use your real name, so a personal greeting doesn’t prove an email is real.
  4. It asks for private info or money. Real companies don’t ask for your password or card details in an email.
  5. A suspicious link or button. Hover over it (or press and hold on a phone) to see where it really goes. If it isn’t the company’s real website, don’t click.
  6. An unexpected attachment. Don’t open files you weren’t expecting, especially .html, .zip, or .pdf files.

Tip: A well-made scam can have none of these signs. If you’re not sure, don’t use anything in the email. Go to the company’s website or app yourself.

What to do

If you get a suspicious email

  1. Don’t click links or open attachments.
  2. Don’t reply.
  3. Check by going to the company’s real website or app yourself, or call the number on your card or their official site.
  4. Report it (see below), then delete it.

If you already clicked

  1. Close the page and don’t type anything else.
  2. If you typed a password, change it right away on the real website, and anywhere else you used it.
  3. Turn on multi-factor authentication (a second step, like a code from an app).
  4. If you gave card or bank info, call your bank using the number on your card.
  5. If you opened a file, run a security scan and tell your school’s or work’s IT team.
  6. Report it.

Where to report it

  • In your email app, use “Report phishing” or “Report junk.”
  • Forward the email to reportphishing@apwg.org (the Anti-Phishing Working Group).
  • Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
  • Forward scam text messages to 7726 (it spells SPAM).
  • If you lost money, call your bank and file a report at ic3.gov.
